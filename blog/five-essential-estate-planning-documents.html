5 Essential Estate Planning Documents to Have

2026-10-31

If you're starting to think about estate planning, you may have heard that there are a handful of "must-have" documents. While every situation is different, most estate plans are built around five core pieces of paperwork. Here's a plain-English look at each one.

1. Last Will and Testament

This is the document most people think of first. It spells out how you want your belongings handled and who you want to make decisions on your behalf after you're gone. Without one, state law decides for you — which rarely matches what someone actually wanted.

2. Durable Power of Attorney

A durable power of attorney lets someone you trust manage your financial and legal affairs if you're unable to do so yourself. This can cover things like paying bills, managing accounts, or signing documents on your behalf. It typically needs to be properly signed and notarized to be valid.

3. Medical Power of Attorney

Similar to the financial version, but focused on healthcare decisions. This document names someone to speak for you medically if you can't speak for yourself. It's one of the more time-sensitive documents to have in place, since medical situations can change quickly.

4. Living Will or Advance Directive

This outlines your wishes for medical treatment in specific situations, such as end-of-life care. It gives guidance to both your medical power of attorney and your healthcare providers, so your loved ones aren't left guessing during a stressful moment.

5. HIPAA Authorization

This simple but often overlooked form allows your designated contacts to actually receive information from doctors or hospitals about your condition. Without it, even a spouse or adult child may be denied basic updates due to privacy rules.

Having the Documents Is Only Half the Job

Drafting these documents is one step. Making sure they're properly signed, notarized, organized, and easy for your trusted contacts to find when it matters is just as important. That's where a lot of families get stuck — paperwork ends up scattered across drawers, old emails, or forgotten files.
